What do teachers and students think of AI?

Methods

Interviews were conducted with six participants (2 teachers, 4 students) from a school in Depok. Participants were selected by the school and interviewed individually. Each interview was conducted offline, lasting about 30-50 minutes, to explore participants’ perspectives regarding AI use in academic settings using a semi-structured format. During interviews, participants were given and signed informed consent before proceeding with the interview. Interview transcripts were reviewed and coded to identify recurring themes, similarities, and differences.

Main Findings

After analyzing the interview results, all participants revealed that the most used AI tools are ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, Deepseek, Claude, Canva AI, Google AI, and Black Box. From the start, many parties in this school repeatedly highlighted the school’s strength in technology integration. Although AI use began in 2020, its integration increased after the school adopted Chromebooks in 2024 and introduced coding classes later on. The introduction of Chromebooks increased access to AI and the coding class incorporates greater AI involvement due to its efficiency. However, all participants expressed concerns that AI may facilitate cheating and potential dependency especially since there was no policy of using AI in the school. Teachers suggested professional guidance to foster responsible utilization, although some have already been restricting Chromebook use in class to prevent AI misuse. Meanwhile, students’ concerns were more about AI’s impact on cognition, privacy, and security. Yet, the findings suggest the possibility of increasing reliance on AI for completing tasks. With technologies already integrated into learning, AI may become more prominent in education. Overall, as AI becomes more common in schools, better guidance is needed to ensure responsible and effective use.

Analysis and Critical Reflection

The interviews revealed concerns regarding potential AI misuse when teachers and students are not fully prepared to use AI critically and responsibly. Although efficient, reliance on AI may reduce essential learning skills. Therefore, precautionary measures are necessary to ensure that AI supports rather than undermines learning development. Several theories can help explain the underlying mechanisms behind the normalization and AI reliance.

Techno-Optimism

The findings suggest techno-optimistic attitudes toward digital learning technologies, which refers to the belief that technological advancement generally improves quality of life, while potentially leading to overreliance on technological solutions (Danaher, 2022). Aligned with this perspective, the school shows an inclination toward technological integration in education, as is evident in Chromebooks’ presence and coding courses. Although these developments do not explicitly indicate full AI integration, they may demonstrate a pipeline toward increasingly digital and AI-assisted learning environments, as they indicate a growing openness toward AI in academic settings due to the optimism (Meivawati et al., 2026).

Lack of Consensus

Based on the interviews, it can be concluded that the participants demonstrated differing views regarding the ethical use of AI. The absence of formal AI policies may contribute to these differing perspectives. AI offers a myriad of applications that make it difficult to narrow down proper structured guidelines, resulting in varying perspectives on how AI should be utilized. Since guidelines for AI utilization have not yet been formalized, there may be risks of normalizing AI which could ultimately lead to dependency.

Cognitive Offloading

Cognitive offloading involves the utilization of external tools or systems to reduce mental effort and cognitive demand by shifting cognitive tasks to those tools (Risko & Gilbert, 2016). Participants reported using AI to search for information, summarize content, generate ideas, and perform calculations, which align with the forms of cognitive offloading. Frequently ‘offloading’ cognitive tasks may ultimately lead to excessive reliance on AI and contribute to reduced critical thinking and independent information processing.

Cognitive Dissonance

Cognitive Dissonance Theory explains the psychological discomfort that occurs when one’s beliefs, attitudes, or behaviors are inconsistent. In this context, participants acknowledged the ethical implications of using AI including feelings of guilt when relying on AI, yet many still use it regularly for academic and professional tasks. These patterns may reflect elements of dissonance, which often leads people to justify their AI use by focusing on its practical benefits or referring to others who also use AI. As a result, attention may shift from integrity-focused concerns to prioritize efficiency (Zheng & Wang, 2026; Ren et al., 2026).

Future Consequences

  • Development of school-based AI systems and standardized tools.
  • Reduced critical evaluation of AI-generated information.
  • Risk of dependency and decline in independent learning skills.

Conclusion and Recommendation

In conclusion, the interviews indicate students' and teachers’ reliance on AI. Furthermore, continuous uncritical integration may increase the risk of future AI dependence. Therefore, formalizing standardized guidelines for AI use should become a necessity. Alongside this, schools may need to prioritize adopting a soft-skill-based strategy known as “SHINE”:

Strategic Thinking

Strategic thinking involves planning and establishing goals to develop purposeful thinking. It requires individuals to be aware of their own thinking. This can be done through self-reflection, a skill that should first be introduced and guided by a more experienced figure. Furthermore, it is important to encourage individuals to consistently practice self-reflection while regularly giving feedback (Drmola & Kraus, 2023; Santrock, 2006).

Hone Critical Thinking

Critical thinking involves reflective and productive thinking when evaluating evidence related to received information. To think critically about AI use, students need direct experience using AI while establishing clear strategies and goals beforehand. Teachers also play an important role in encouraging students to question the reliability of AI-generated information through thought-provoking questions, reasonable argumentation, and evidence-based discussion (Santrock, 2006; Dessingué & Wagner, 2025).

Implement Project-Based Learning

Some students admitted to relying heavily on AI when completing tasks, especially annual projects. Implementing project-based learning enables collaboration among students, teachers, and communities as a method to facilitate thought processes. Here, teachers can ask critical follow-up questions to clarify students’ understanding and ensure active participation (Santrock, 2006; Krajcik & Blumenfeld, 2006; Andersen et al., 2006). Therefore, this encourages students to grasp the materials being learned beyond relying on AI, while keeping human authenticity in the making process.

Nurture a Mastery-Oriented Mindset

As educators, teachers should model enjoyment in the learning process and foster students’ engagement in learning, instead of emphasizing outcomes (i.e., grade score, recognition). When students feel more motivated and challenged, they are more likely to achieve their goals by themselves, instead of letting AI do all the work (Santrock, 2006; Schiefele, 2017).

Encourage Intrinsic Motivation

The ultimate goal is to increase students’ reliance on intrinsic motivation. When students are intrinsically motivated, they are more likely to regulate their own AI use and remain mindful of its potential drawbacks. Over time, students may develop an understanding of responsible AI use while still receiving guidance and support from teachers (Santrock, 2006).
